# संस्कृतम् in English · Sanskrit's Quiet Imprint on English
## About 100 everyday English words with Sanskrit roots: how they travelled, what they tell us, and which origins are still debated

Pour a glass of orange juice, stir in some sugar, nibble a piece of ginger candy, and dream of a walk in the jungle. Without noticing, you have just used five words with Sanskrit ancestry. Sanskrit's influence on English is real, but most of it is invisible. The words arrived in disguise, after long journeys through other languages, and by the time they reached London they looked and sounded completely English.

This article follows those journeys. It is written for parents, teachers, and older children who enjoy the detective work of etymology (the study of where words come from). Each Sanskrit root is given in Devanagari with its IAST transliteration, for example शर्करा (śarkarā). Where scholars are unsure, we say so.

## Three Roads into English

Sanskrit words did not come into English all at once. They came along three broad roads, in different centuries, for different reasons.

## Road 1 · The Ancient Trade Route

For more than two thousand years, Indian goods such as spices, sugar, dyes, perfumes, and gems travelled west. Their names travelled with them, passed from language to language like a parcel: from Sanskrit into Prakrit or Pali, then Persian, Arabic, Greek, Latin, and Old French, and finally into English. Each language reshaped the word a little, so the Sanskrit original is often hard to recognise.

- Sugar: शर्करा (śarkarā, 'grit, ground sugar') → Persian shakar → Arabic sukkar → Italian zucchero → Old French sucre → English sugar.
- Crimson and carmine: कृमिज (kṛmija, 'produced by a worm') from कृमि (kṛmi, 'worm'), because the red dye was made from tiny insects. The word travelled through Persian and Arabic qirmiz into medieval Latin and Spanish.
- Ginger: शृङ्गवेर (śṛṅgavera, 'horn-shaped root') → Prakrit siṅgabera → Greek zingiberis → Latin zingiber → Old English gingifer. The Sanskrit form may itself have been reshaped from a Dravidian word.
- Orange: नारङ्ग (nāraṅga) → Persian nārang → Arabic nāranj → Old French orenge. The fruit gave its name to the colour, not the other way round.
- Musk: मुष्क (muṣka) → Persian mushk → Greek moskhos → Latin muscus. The word named the scent pod of the musk deer.

## Road 2 · The Colonial Route (17th Century Onwards)

From the 1600s, British traders, soldiers, and officials of the East India Company lived in India and picked up words from Hindi, Urdu, Marathi, Bengali, and other Indian languages, many of which descend from Sanskrit. These words came into English more directly, so they are easier to recognise.

- Bungalow: Hindi baṅglā, 'a house in the Bengal style', from वङ्ग (Vaṅga, Bengal).
- Loot: Hindi lūṭ, from the Sanskrit root लुण्ठ् (luṇṭh-, 'to rob').
- Thug: Hindi ṭhag, 'swindler', probably from स्थग (sthaga, 'cunning').
- Jungle: Hindi jaṅgal, from जङ्गल (jaṅgala, 'dry, uncultivated land'). The meaning changed completely: the Sanskrit word described dry wasteland, but in English it came to mean thick tropical forest.
- Shampoo: Hindi cā̃po, 'press! knead!' (a head-and-body massage), usually traced to चपयति (capayati, 'kneads').
- Bandana: Hindi bāndhnū, 'tie-dyed cloth', from बन्ध् (bandh-, 'to tie').
- Cot: Hindi khāṭ, from खट्वा (khaṭvā, 'bedstead').
- Titles such as rajah (राजन् rājan, 'king'), pundit (पण्डित paṇḍita, 'learned'), and guru (गुरु guru, 'weighty; teacher').

A word of caution: pyjama is often listed as a Sanskrit word, but it is not. It comes from Persian pāy-jāma ('leg garment') and reached English through Hindi and Urdu. It is an Indian loanword, but not a Sanskrit one.

## Road 3 · The Spiritual and Scholarly Wave (19th–20th Centuries)

In the 1800s and 1900s, European scholars translated the Vedas, the Upaniṣads, the Bhagavad Gītā, and Buddhist texts, and later yoga teachers and spiritual travellers brought Indian ideas to the West. This third wave brought words such as yoga, karma, nirvana, mantra, avatar, dharma, and mandala.

What makes this group special is that their meanings stayed close to the original. Sugar has forgotten that it was once शर्करा (śarkarā), but when people say karma today they still mean something close to कर्मन् (karman, 'action and its consequences'). Some meanings have loosened in everyday speech: an avatar is now also a profile picture, and a mantra can be a company slogan. Even so, the link to the Sanskrit idea remains clear.

## Borrowing vs Cognates: Family Resemblance Is Not Borrowing

In 1786, Sir William Jones, a judge in Calcutta, told the Asiatic Society that Sanskrit, Greek, and Latin were so alike in their roots and grammar that they must have "sprung from some common source." This insight led to the discovery of the Indo-European language family, which includes Sanskrit, Persian, Greek, Latin, and the Germanic languages, English among them.

This means there are two very different ways a Sanskrit word can resemble an English one:

- Borrowing: English took the word, directly or through other languages, at some point in recorded history. Sugar, jungle, and yoga are borrowings.
- Cognates: both languages inherited the word from their shared ancestor, Proto-Indo-European, spoken perhaps 6,000 years ago. Neither language borrowed from the other; they are cousins, not parent and child.

| Sanskrit | English | Latin / Greek | Shared meaning |
| मातृ (mātṛ) | mother | Latin māter | mother |
| पितृ (pitṛ) | father | Latin pater | father |
| भ्रातृ (bhrātṛ) | brother | Latin frāter | brother |
| नव (nava) | nine | Latin novem | nine |

Similarly, दन्त (danta, 'tooth') is a cousin of Latin dēns, from which English gets dental and dentist, and of English tooth itself. Words like mother, father, and the numbers are cognates, not borrowings, so they are not in our list. Here is a neat twist: the English word pal is a real borrowing from भ्रातृ (bhrātṛ), carried by the Romani language, while brother is its ancient cousin.

The 100-word list below contains only borrowings: words that actually travelled from Sanskrit into English.

## What the Categories Reveal

Grouping the borrowings by theme tells a story about the contact between India and the English-speaking world.

- Trade goods came first and travelled furthest: sugar, pepper, ginger, camphor, sandalwood, musk, lac, and indigo. Their names reached Europe centuries before any English person visited India.
- Administration and colonial life brought titles (rajah, maharani, pundit) and the vocabulary of rule and law and order. Two words took darker meanings in English: thug, from a word for a swindler, became a general word for a violent criminal, and juggernaut, from जगन्नाथ (Jagannātha, 'Lord of the World', the deity of Puri's chariot festival), came to mean an unstoppable, crushing force, based on exaggerated European reports.
- Domestic words show how closely people lived together: cot, shampoo, bangle, bandana, bungalow, chit, and gunny sack.
- Spiritual and philosophical terms arrived last. They are the only group where English kept the original meaning almost intact, because people borrowed them precisely for their meaning.

## What We Left Out, and Why

A careful list is also about what does not belong. Some words that often appear in "Sanskrit in English" lists were removed after fact-checking:

- Pyjama: Persian pāy-jāma, reaching English through Hindi and Urdu, not Sanskrit.
- Rice: not a Sanskrit borrowing. Greek oryza is at most distantly related to Sanskrit व्रीहि (vrīhi).
- Crocus: of Semitic origin; the proposed link to कुङ्कुम (kuṅkuma, saffron) is not accepted.
- Das and chakram: Indian words, but not established English words.
- Nark: from Romani nāk ('nose'); the Sanskrit link is too weak.
- Gharry: from Hindi gāṛī ('cart'); the proposed Sanskrit root is doubtful.
- Aryan: left out because the English word, originally from आर्य (ārya, 'noble'), was later misused in racial theories. That history deserves a careful discussion of its own.

## Closing: A Substrate Hiding in Plain Sight

Sanskrit never conquered English, and English speakers rarely studied it until the eighteenth century. Yet its words are everywhere, in the kitchen (sugar, ginger, dal), the wardrobe (sari, shawl, cashmere), the zoo (cheetah, jackal, langur), the bathroom (shampoo), and the yoga studio (karma, mantra, chakra). Some came with merchants, some with soldiers and clerks, and some with scholars and seekers.

Next time you peel an orange or call a friend your pal, remember that you are speaking a little Sanskrit. The words have travelled a long way, but their roots are still there, quietly waiting for curious readers to notice them.
