# दशगुणोत्तरसंज्ञाः महासङ्ख्याश्च · Naming the Colossal: How Indian Texts Counted Past a Million
## From the Yajurveda (10¹²) and Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa (10⁵⁷) to the Lalitavistara (10¹⁴⁰), Līlāvatī (10¹⁷), and Jaina Infinities

Carl Sagan, in Cosmos (Random House, 1980), chapter 10, “The Edge of Forever,” made a famous observation about the scale of Indian thought:
> "The Hindu religion is the only one of the world’s great faiths dedicated to the idea that the Cosmos itself undergoes an immense, indeed an infinite, number of deaths and rebirths. It is the only religion in which the time scales correspond, no doubt by accident, to those of modern scientific cosmology. Its cycles run from our ordinary day and night to a day and night of Brahma, 8.64 billion years long, longer than the age of the Earth or the Sun and about half the time since the Big Bang. And there are much longer time scales still."

This cosmic imagination was not merely poetic: it directly fostered a culture of extreme mathematical numeration. While ancient Greece stopped naming numbers at the myriad (10⁴ = 10,000) and Rome stopped at mille (10³ = 1,000), ancient Indian scholars named powers of ten reaching 10¹², 10⁵⁷, and 10¹⁴⁰, before pioneering the mathematics of infinity itself.

## 1 · Vedic Number Names: The Thirteen Decuple Rungs

The Vājasaneyi Saṃhitā of the White Yajurveda (17.2), also preserved in the Taittirīya Saṃhitā (4.4.11.4) and Maitrāyaṇī Saṃhitā, documents a systematic thirteen-step decuple ladder where each named rank is ten times the preceding one (*daśaguṇottaram*):

| Rank | Sanskrit Name | IAST | Value (Power of 10) | Modern English Equivalent |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | **एक** | eka | 10⁰ | One |
| 2 | **दश** | daśa | 10¹ | Ten |
| 3 | **शत** | śata | 10² | Hundred |
| 4 | **सहस्र** | sahasra | 10³ | Thousand |
| 5 | **अयुत** | ayuta | 10⁴ | Ten Thousand |
| 6 | **नियुत** | niyuta | 10⁵ | Hundred Thousand (Lakh) |
| 7 | **प्रयुत** | prayuta | 10⁶ | Million |
| 8 | **अर्बुद** | arbuda | 10⁷ | Ten Million (Crore) |
| 9 | **न्यर्बुद** | nyarbuda | 10⁸ | Hundred Million |
| 10 | **समुद्र** | samudra | 10⁹ | One Billion |
| 11 | **मध्य** | madhya | 10¹⁰ | Ten Billion |
| 12 | **अन्त** | anta | 10¹¹ | Hundred Billion |
| 13 | **परार्ध** | parārdha | 10¹² | One Trillion |

In this Vedic enumeration, each step advances tenfold, culminating in **Parārdha** as 10¹² (one trillion). In later classical treatises, such as Śrīdhara’s *Pāṭīgaṇita* and Bhāskarācārya’s *Līlāvatī*, the standard decuple ladder was extended to eighteen places, assigning the name **Parārdha** to the 18th place (10¹⁷). Both scales reflect the same foundational decimal architecture: naming ascending powers of ten with structured mathematical clarity.

## 2 · The Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa Census: Counting the Vānara Army to 10⁵⁷

In the Yuddha Kāṇḍa (Book 6, Canto 28, Verses 33–42) of the Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Śuka, the chief spy of Rāvaṇa, returns from scouting the forces of Rāma and Sugrīva. When Rāvaṇa demands to know the exact strength of the invading army, Śuka recites an astonishing exponential table advancing by factors of 100,000 (एकलक्ष):

- **शतम् (Śatam)** = 10² = 100
- **सहस्रम् (Sahasram)** = 10³ = 1,000
- **लक्षम् (Lakṣam)** = 10⁵ = 100,000
- **कोटिः (Koṭi)** = 10⁷ = 100 Lakṣa = 10,000,000
- **शङ्कुः (Śaṅku)** = 100,000 Koṭi = 10¹²
- **महाशङ्कुः (Mahāśaṅku)** = 100,000 Śaṅku = 10¹⁷
- **वृन्दम् (Vṛnda)** = 100,000 Mahāśaṅku = 10²²
- **महावृन्दम् (Mahāvṛnda)** = 100,000 Vṛnda = 10²⁷
- **पद्मम् (Padma)** = 100,000 Mahāvṛnda = 10³²
- **महापद्मम् (Mahāpadma)** = 100,000 Padma = 10³⁷
- **खर्वम् (Kharva)** = 100,000 Mahāpadma = 10⁴²
- **महाखर्वम् (Mahākharva)** = 100,000 Kharva = 10⁴⁷
- **समुद्रः (Samudra)** = 100,000 Mahākharva = 10⁵²
- **ओघः (Ogha)** = 100,000 Samudra = 10⁵⁷
- **महौघः (Mahaugha)** = 100,000 Ogha = 10⁶²

Śuka explains that Sugrīva’s army consists of "one thousand Śaṅku, one hundred Mahāśaṅku, a thousand Vṛnda, an ocean of Mahāvṛnda..." Demonstrating that huge exponential numbers were naturally embedded in classical Sanskrit literature.

## 3 · The Lalitavistara: Tallakṣaṇa (10⁵³) & Cosmic Atom Counting (10¹⁴⁰)

In Chapter 12 of the Buddhist Lalitavistara Sūtra, Prince Siddhārtha (Gautama Buddha) engages in a legendary mathematical contest against the royal master mathematician Arjuna. Arjuna asks: "Prince, canst thou recite the numeration running past a hundred Koṭis?"

Siddhārtha recites a centesimal (100-fold) ladder:
- 100 Koṭi = 1 Ayuta (10⁹)
- 100 Ayuta = 1 Niyuta (10¹¹)
- 100 Niyuta = 1 Kaṅkara (10¹³)
- 100 Kaṅkara = 1 Vivara (10¹⁵)
- 100 Vivara = 1 Akṣobhya (10¹⁷)
- 100 Akṣobhya = 1 Vivāha (10¹⁹)
... ascending through 23 centesimal steps to 100 Vibhūtaṅgama = 1 **Tallakṣaṇa** (तल्लक्षण = 10⁵³).

Siddhārtha explains that Tallakṣaṇa is merely the first scale (*Prathama Gaṇanā*). He continues into subsequent ladders designed to count every grain of sand along the Gaṅgā, the raindrops falling across all worlds, and the microscopic atoms of the cosmos, culminating in the supreme numeration: **Uttaraparamāṇurajaḥpraveśa** (उत्तरपरमाणुरजःप्रवेश = 10¹⁴⁰).

> **Cosmological Perspective**: Modern astrophysics estimates that the entire observable universe contains roughly 10⁸⁰ subatomic particles (protons, neutrons, electrons). Ancient Indian mathematicians had named, classified, and manipulated numbers exceeding the universe’s total particle count by sixty orders of magnitude!

## 4 · Bhāskarācārya’s 18 Decimal Places: The Līlāvatī Standard (1150 CE)

In his mathematical treatise Līlāvatī (verses 10–11), Bhāskarācārya II codified the eighteen canonical decimal place values (Aṣṭādaśa Sthānāni) utilized by classical astronomers and merchants:

1. **एक (Eka)** = 10⁰ = Units
2. **दश (Daśa)** = 10¹ = Tens
3. **शत (Śata)** = 10² = Hundreds
4. **सहस्र (Sahasra)** = 10³ = Thousands
5. **अयुत (Ayuta)** = 10⁴ = Ten Thousands
6. **लक्ष (Lakṣa)** = 10⁵ = Lakh
7. **प्रयुत (Prayuta)** = 10⁶ = Million
8. **कोटि (Koṭi)** = 10⁷ = Crore
9. **अर्बुद (Arbuda)** = 10⁸ = Ten Crore
10. **अब्ज / पद्म (Abja / Padma)** = 10⁹ = Billion
11. **खर्व (Kharva)** = 10¹⁰ = Ten Billion
12. **निखर्व (Nikharva)** = 10¹¹ = Hundred Billion
13. **महापद्म (Mahāpadma)** = 10¹² = Trillion
14. **शङ्कु (Śaṅku)** = 10¹³ = Ten Trillion
15. **जलधि / समुद्र (Jaladhi / Samudra)** = 10¹⁴ = Hundred Trillion
16. **अन्त्य (Antya)** = 10¹⁵ = Quadrillion
17. **मध्य (Madhya)** = 10¹⁶ = Ten Quadrillion
18. **परार्ध (Parārdha)** = 10¹⁷ = Hundred Quadrillion

## 5 · Jaina Infinities: Countable, Innumerable, and the Infinite

Jaina mathematicians of the 3rd to 2nd centuries BCE made a profound philosophical and mathematical breakthrough: they were the first thinkers in human history to recognize that infinity is not a single monolith, but contains distinct mathematical orders and cardinalities—anticipating Georg Cantor (1874 CE) by two millennia!

The Anuyogadvāra Sūtra divides all quantities into three categories:
1. **Saṅkhyāta (संख्यात)**: Countable finite numbers.
2. **Asaṅkhyāta (असंख्यात)**: Innumerable quantities (numbers beyond calculation but still strictly finite, such as grains of sand in all oceans).
3. **Ananta (अनन्त)**: Truly infinite quantities.

Crucially, the Jaina texts subdivide Ananta into distinct classes:
- **Ananta**: Infinite in one direction or dimension.
- **Dvi-ananta**: Infinite in two directions.
- **Samananta**: Infinite in area.
- **Anantānanta (अनन्तानन्त)**: An infinity of infinities (transfinite infinity).
- **Time-infinities (Kāla-ananta)** vs. **Space-infinities (Pradeśa-ananta)**.

## Summary Checklist
- **Vājasaneyi Saṃhitā (17.2)**: 13-step decuple ladder from Eka (10⁰) to Parārdha (10¹²).
- **Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa (6.28)**: 100,000-fold multiplier steps reaching Ogha (10⁵⁷) and Mahaugha (10⁶²).
- **Lalitavistara Sūtra (ch. 12)**: Centesimal ladder to Tallakṣaṇa (10⁵³) and cosmic atom count Uttaraparamāṇurajaḥpraveśa (10¹⁴⁰).
- **Bhāskarācārya’s Līlāvatī (1150 CE)**: 18 canonical decimal places up to Parārdha (10¹⁷).
- **Jaina Anuyogadvāra Sūtra**: Pioneer of multiple transfinite cardinalities (Anantānanta) 2,000 years before Georg Cantor.
